How early can a camera call a three?

Once the ball leaves the hand, physics has already decided where it lands. What is left is a measuring problem: how many frames does a camera need before it knows what the rim will do? This page builds the whole chain in simulation, from pixels to a probability, and times it.

A miss can be called early and a make cannot. From one camera, a shot that is clearly short or long gives itself away within a few tenths of a second, and when the model calls a miss it is right of the time. A shot that looks good keeps one secret until the end: whether it is drifting left or right, toward or away from the lens. A second camera at an angle sees that too.

Why the make is the hard call

A camera measures two of the ball's three directions well. The third, toward and away from the lens, has to come from weak hints: how big the ball looks and how its arc bends. From the main broadcast camera a shot from the top of the arc flies across the picture, so short or long shows early while left or right hides in depth. From the corners it is the other way round.

A miss needs only one thing to be wrong. A make needs everything to be right, including the part the camera sees worst. The table shows how well one camera separates makes from misses by where the shot was taken, on the same 0.5 to 1 scale as above.

How it works

  1. Find the ballA small convolutional network reads each crop and returns the ball's centre to a fraction of a pixel, its size, its own error bars, and whether a hand still has it. Typical error on the filmed shots: of a pixel.
  2. Spot the releaseA change-point test on two clues, the hand and whether the ball is still gaining speed, picks the frame the ball left the fingers. On average it knows after it happened.
  3. Fit the flightGravity, drag and spin lift are known, so nine numbers pin down a shot. A Bayesian fit finds them from the frames so far, with priors on where shooters stand and aim, and says how unsure it is.
  4. Ask the rimA network trained on simulated arrivals knows what rim, glass and backspin do to a ball coming in a given way. The fit's uncertainty goes through it to give a probability.
  5. Check the confidenceWith one camera the fit grows too sure of itself late in the flight. The last step widens its uncertainty by a factor learned from the outcomes of shots held out for that.

Why not just train a network on it?

A fair question, so it was tried. A recurrent network was given what the main camera measures, already turned into angles with the camera's calibration, told the exact frame of the release, and trained on shots to say make or miss at every frame. Then it and the physics fit called the same new shots, filmed by an ideal detector so that neither was helped or hurt by pictures.

It gets most of the way and then stalls. For the first few frames the two are level. From there the fit pulls ahead, because the last stretch is decided by centimetres at the rim, and those come from equations the fit does not have to learn. More data and a bigger network would narrow the gap. The fit also moves to a new camera position with nothing but that camera's calibration, where a network would need labelled shots filmed from there.

Could you bet on it?

No. At best the model is about a second ahead of the ball. A TV picture reaches a viewer several seconds late and a stream later still, sportsbooks hold in-play bets for a few seconds before taking them, and their own courtside data arrives faster than any broadcast. Whoever could act on a call this early is already sitting on the fast feed.

What is real here and what is not

  • Everything is simulated: the shots, the cameras and the pictures. No game footage was used, and none of these numbers has been checked against a real broadcast.
  • The physics is standard: gravity, air drag, lift from backspin, and bounces with friction off rim and glass. Shooter error is tuned so that of threes go in. Real rims, nets and shooters are messier.
  • The detector learned from drawn basketballs. On real frames, compression, hands, bodies and camera cuts would cost it accuracy.
  • The cameras are assumed calibrated to about a pixel. In a real broadcast that is a hard problem of its own.
  • So trust the shape of the result more than its decimals: misses can be called early, makes cannot, and a second angle changes that.
